SPARC
Scene-Guided Photon Acquisition and Sparse Representation for SP-LiDAR Compression

Hashan K. Weerasooriya, Weijian Zhang, Stanley H. Chan
School of Electrical and Computer Engineering, Purdue University, West Lafayette, IN, USA
IEEE/CVF Winter Conference on Applications of Computer Vision (WACV) 2027 · Applications Track

Abstract

Single-Photon LiDAR (SP-LiDAR) provides exceptional depth sensitivity but produces massive spatio-temporal data volumes that hinder real-time deployment. Existing methods primarily reduce data volume after spatially dense photon acquisition, relying on rigid sensing strategies or scene-agnostic temporal coding that cannot reduce the sensing burden itself. We propose SPARC (Scene-guided Photon Acquisition and Sparse Representation for SP-LiDAR Compression), an acquisition-aware compression framework that moves compression decisions ahead of acquisition, choosing where photon measurements are captured and how retained waveforms are represented. SPARC uses low-cost RGB scene priors to guide adaptive photon acquisition, then encodes the retained measurements using a learned sparsifying transform into compact coefficient–index pairs. A lightweight reconstruction network, sparseSPADnet, directly recovers dense depth and reflectivity from the sparse representation without reconstructing a dense histogram cube. Experiments on simulated driving scenes, real SP-LiDAR measurements, and embedded deployment on an NVIDIA Jetson AGX Xavier demonstrate that SPARC consistently outperforms conventional fixed-basis temporal coding across diverse photon-flux conditions while maintaining a compact computational footprint. These results demonstrate the potential of acquisition-aware compression for scalable, resource-efficient SP-LiDAR sensing.

Why Compression Happens Too Late

In SP-LiDAR each pixel accumulates photon arrivals across thousands of laser pulses, producing a 3D histogram cube \(\mathcal{H} \in \mathbb{R}^{W \times H \times N_{\text{bins}}}\) that scales with both spatial resolution and temporal bin count. Even at 8 bits per bin, a \(128 \times 256 \times 1024\) cube is about 33.5 MB per frame.

Existing work largely compresses those histograms after acquisition. Hardware approaches reduce transmission through compact histogramming or adaptive scanning, but rely on rigid sensing strategies or costly sequential scene exploration that introduces a cold-start overhead. Algorithmic methods apply sketching, compressive coding, or learned representations to already-acquired histograms — they assume dense, scene-agnostic acquisition and therefore cannot reduce the sensing burden itself.

The disconnect. Existing methods optimize how dense measurements are represented while treating where measurements are acquired as fixed. An SP-LiDAR system should decide both. A low-cost co-located RGB camera provides the scene information that makes acquisition-aware sensing practical.

Contributions

  1. Acquisition-aware SP-LiDAR compression.
    RGB-guided adaptive photon acquisition allocates the photon budget before histogram formation, reducing both the sensing burden and the amount of data that must be represented and transmitted.
  2. Decoder-independent learned sparse representation.
    A learned temporal sparsifying transform represents retained waveforms with compact coefficient–index pairs. It is learned independently of the reconstruction network and held fixed at deployment, so the payload can be decoded by different estimators without retraining the compression stage. The transform stays compact across low-flux, pile-up and dead-time conditions.
  3. Lightweight joint reconstruction and system validation.
    sparseSPADnet reconstructs dense depth and reflectivity directly from the sparse representation, without materializing the histogram cube. The framework is validated on simulated and real photon measurements and runs on an NVIDIA Jetson AGX Xavier.

Method

SPARC runs in three stages: RGB-guided spatial acquisition, temporal compression with a pre-learned sparsifying transform, and neural reconstruction. Spatial and temporal compression are applied sequentially to the histogram cube \(\mathcal{H}\), and the resulting sparse payload \(\mathcal{P}\) is decoded straight into the scene estimate \(\hat{\mathcal{S}}\) — a pair of joint depth and reflectivity maps.

Compression \(\mathcal{P} = \text{Compress}_{\text{temp}}\big(\text{Compress}_{\text{spatial}}(\mathcal{H})\big)\)
Decoding \(\hat{\mathcal{S}} = \text{Decode}(\mathcal{P})\)

Spatial Compression via RGB-Guided Sampling

Neighboring pixels have highly correlated temporal responses, so spreading a fixed photon budget uniformly is wasteful. SPARC uses an RGB image captured before acquisition to estimate a spatial importance \(I(u,v) \ge 0\), sampling pixel \((u,v)\) with probability \(p(u,v) = I(u,v) / \sum_{u,v} I(u,v)\). Only sampled locations are measured, so the dense cube is never formed — compression happens at the sensing stage. Two strategies are supported: texture-aware sampling, \(I_{\text{tex}} = \alpha_g G + \alpha_v V\) from gradient magnitude and local intensity variance, which preserves object boundaries and fine structure; and object-aware sampling, which reserves part of the budget for detector bounding boxes and is used in the embedded evaluation.

Temporal Compression via a Learned Sparsifying Transform

SPARC treats temporal compression as an explicit sparse-representation problem rather than an end-to-end latent code. A square analysis transform \(\mathbf{W}\) maps each histogram \(\mathbf{y}\) to \(\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{W}\mathbf{y}\), keeping only the \(s \ll N_{\text{bins}}\) largest coefficients via hard thresholding \(\mathcal{T}_s\). Transform and codes are learned jointly by solving \(\min_{\mathbf{W},\mathbf{X}} \|\mathbf{W}\mathbf{Y} - \mathbf{X}\|_F^2 + \lambda_1 \Phi(\mathbf{W})\) subject to \(\|\mathbf{x}_i\|_0 \le s\) for every histogram, with \(\Phi(\mathbf{W}) = \|\mathbf{W}\|_F^2 - \gamma \log\det(\mathbf{W})\) keeping \(\mathbf{W}\) full-rank. Unlike Fourier or wavelet bases, the learned atoms adapt to detector-specific distortions — pile-up, dead-time, low-flux — so \(s = 4\) coefficient–index pairs suffice where fixed bases fail. Atom pruning gives a 10× reduction in dimensionality, and because the transform is learned offline and then held fixed, the payload stays independent of the reconstruction network.

The atoms are not matched filters. Rather than correlating against one fixed template to localize a peak, the transform projects each histogram onto a small set of regime-adaptive atoms that jointly encode signal, background, and detector distortion. Only \(s = 4\) atoms are active in every regime above, and pruning leaves as few as 79 of \(N_{\text{bins}} = 1024\).

Reconstruction: sparseSPADnet

sparseSPADnet decodes the payload directly from the atom-index tensor \(\mathbf{A}\) and coefficient tensor \(\mathbf{V}\), zero-padded at unsampled locations; trained independently of the transform, it can be replaced without relearning the compression stage. Indices and coefficients carry complementary information, so each is embedded and summed into a token \(\mathbf{T}_i = E_{\text{atom}}(\mathbf{A}_i) + E_{\text{val}}(\mathbf{V}_i)\). Because the retained atoms are unordered, attention-based pooling learns their relative importance and aggregates them into one dense feature. A parallel intensity-proxy branch adds the local intensity cues the atoms lack; the fused features pass through a lightweight U-Net and two task-specific heads for joint depth and reflectivity.

Embedded Deployment

The complete acquisition–compression–reconstruction pipeline is deployed on an NVIDIA Jetson AGX Xavier (32 TOPS, 30 W). Here the objective is embedded autonomous sensing rather than scene-wide fidelity, so SPARC runs in object-aware mode: a TensorRT-optimized YOLOv11-nano detector identifies object regions and a constant-velocity Kalman filter predicts locations between frames, generating stable acquisition masks before photon sensing begins. This predictive strategy concentrates the photon budget on dynamically relevant regions while reducing spatial jitter from per-frame detection variability.

Because SPARC generates the sparse payload directly from adaptive spatial acquisition combined with learned temporal encoding, a spatially dense histogram cube is never materialized — for a \(128 \times 256 \times 1024\) cube this avoids transferring about 33.5 MB per frame even at 8 bits per bin. Sparse encoding is a single matrix multiplication followed by top-\(s\) thresholding, after which the payload is passed straight to a TensorRT FP32 implementation of sparseSPADnet; one forward pass yields dense depth and reflectivity with no post-processing.

System performance. Core compute latency is approximately 265 ms — 125 ms for sparseSPADnet inference, 100 ms for sparse encoding, and 40 ms for object detection and Kalman filtering. The deployment consumes an average GPU power of 3.5 W (16.0 W total board) with a peak unified memory usage of 7.5 GB. A further ≈235 ms of disk I/O in the offline evaluation disappears in a live system, where photon acquisition and data transfer overlap with computation from the previous frame.
